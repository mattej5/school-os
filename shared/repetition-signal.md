# Repetition signal — what a professor repeats, they test

When analyzing lecture slides, recordings, or any professor-authored material, treat repetition as a weighting signal, not as padding to be discounted.

## The principle

If a slide deck (or a professor across multiple lectures) restates the same concept, model, or term more than once — the same framework shown again with a different animation, a definition repeated in a later session, a point made once in lecture and again in a review slide — that is the professor telling you what matters. Professors don't repeat things at random; repetition is usually deliberate emphasis, and deliberate emphasis correlates with what shows up on exams and in graded questions.

## How to apply it

- When assessing the richness or density of course material, don't just count "substantive vs. filler" pages. Flag *which* substantive content recurs across slides, sessions, or decks within a course.
- Weight recurring topics higher than single-mention topics when:
  - Prioritizing what to quiz on (**adaptive-quiz**)
  - Ranking what to study first (**weakness-tracker**, Mode 1)
  - Building a study guide's emphasis (**study-guide-cowriter**)
- A concept mentioned once in passing is lower priority than one repeated 2-3+ times, even if the single mention looked more "detailed" in isolation.
- Don't confuse simple visual/formatting repetition (the same title-slide template every session) with content repetition (the same named concept, model, or term recurring). Only content repetition is the signal.
- When repetition spans multiple sessions/decks rather than within one deck, that's an even stronger signal — it means the professor is building on or returning to the concept over time.

## Why

A single dense slide can look rich but only ever gets mentioned once — that's a lower-probability exam item than a concept the professor returns to three times across the semester, even if each individual mention is brief.
